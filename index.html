Section A: The Big Idea

A pitch is aimed at a person

Here is the situation. You are on the job or in your program, in your field: a shop, a hangar, a pharmacy, a hospital unit, a salon, a lab, an IT help desk. You see a problem. A piece of equipment, a tool, or a piece of software would fix it. It costs real money. Somebody has to say yes.

That somebody is a person. Not a committee. Not a grading rubric. A person with a job, a budget, habits, and a point of view.

Two pitches to the same boss

Say two people pitch the same $4,000 machine to the same shop owner. We will call the owner Ray.

Pitch A opens with a spec sheet. Horsepower, cycle times, a comparison chart of three brands. Every number is right.

Pitch B opens with something else. The person knows Ray lost a week of work two years ago when a rushed purchase broke down and the dealer would not return calls. So Pitch B opens with: "Here is what happens if this machine goes down, and here is the warranty and the local service tech who will come out." Then the numbers.

Ray says "let me think about it" to Pitch A. He says "when can we get it?" to Pitch B. Both had good data. Only one was built around what Ray worries about.

That is the whole idea of this week. Data matters, but data does not make the decision. A person does. If you know what that person cares about, what worries them, and how they like to hear things, you can build a pitch they will listen to.

What the fancy name means

The class calls this the Rhetorical Situation. In plain words it is six questions you answer before you write anything:

  1. Your Role. Who are you, and why should anyone listen to you on this?
  2. Your Supervisor. Who exactly are you convincing, and how do they think?
  3. The Problem and the Ask. What is going wrong, what does it cost, and what do you want approved?
  4. The Situation on the Ground. What is going on around this request: money, timing, history?
  5. The Equipment or Tool. What are you proposing, and why does each feature matter?
  6. The Rules of the Proposal. How is the proposal written, and how long is it?
Meet Dani

To make this real, you will follow one example person through every step. Dani is an apprentice at a four-bay auto shop. The shop has one alignment rack. Dani wants the owner, Ray, to approve a second alignment machine, around $4,000. Dani is made up, and so are the numbers. Your field will be different. Dani works in an auto shop, but the same steps apply in a weld shop, a diesel bay, a hangar, a machine shop, a pharmacy, a hospital unit, a salon, an IT help desk, or a drafting department. You will build your own with your own real details.

Over the next three weeks you build your proposal one piece at a time. This week is the foundation. Everything after it sits on top of what you work out here. You will not see the Rhetorical Situation Profile worksheet until later in the week. This interactive reading is how you get ready for it, so take it seriously.

Where this goes: This week sets up the rest of the proposal. Week 2 is research. Week 3 puts it all together.

Section B: Step 1 of 6

Your Role: why should they listen to you?

Step 1 asks one question: who are you in this workplace, and why are you the one bringing this up?

Your supervisor hears pitches all the time. If your role sounds vague, they have no reason to take you seriously. What gives you standing is what you actually do and what you see because of it.

Standing is not your title. "Employee" tells them nothing. "Apprentice" tells them a little. What you do, how often, and how many tells them a lot.

What standing is built from

  • What you do. A real task, with numbers. How many, how often, how long.
  • What you see. Because of your job, you notice things other people do not. The person on the floor sees the problem first. That is your reason for speaking up.
  • Your background. A real piece of who you are that shapes how you show up at work. Where you grew up, what you did before, who taught you the trade.

The worksheet also asks for your name and who you are, your job title, and how long you have held it. Those ground you. The role field is where the detail goes.

Watch it work: Dani, Step 1
Dani's first draft"I'm a tech at the shop. I work hard and I like cars."
Dani's revision"I'm Dani Ruiz, a second-year apprentice at a four-bay shop. I do about eight alignments a week, and I keep the rack schedule, so I'm the one who sees cars stack up when the rack is busy. I grew up helping my dad on his farm equipment, so I'm careful about anything that has to be done right."
Why the revision works: it has a task (alignments), a number (eight a week), something Dani sees that others might not (cars stacking up), and a piece of background that explains how Dani approaches work.

Doing this well

  • Name something you actually do, not just a title.
  • Use numbers wherever you can: how many, how often, how long.
  • Say what you see that other people do not, because of your role. That is your standing to bring this forward.
  • Include a real piece of your background that shapes how you show up at work.
  • Be honest. Your supervisor knows what you actually do. Stretching it costs you credibility the moment they notice.

If you do not have a job right now, use your classes, a past job, a team, a club, or volunteer work. What do you actually do there, how often, and what have you noticed that others miss?

Where this goes: Step 1 does not get its own section in the paper. It shapes how credible you sound everywhere in the paper.

Section C: Step 2 of 6, Part A

Your Supervisor: pick a real person

Step 2 is the most important step. You are building a profile of the person you must convince.

Here is the rule: your supervisor cannot be invented. Pick a real person you actually know. A parent, a current or former manager, a coach, a teacher. Then imagine them as your future boss in the field you are going into.

Why a real person? Because you cannot guess what a made-up boss would care about. You can answer honestly about someone you know. And the whole point of this course is learning to read a real person.

How to choose

  • Pick someone you know well. You should be able to answer "what would they say if I walked in with this?" without guessing.
  • Pick someone who would actually have to be convinced. If your pick says yes to everything, you learn nothing about how to make a real case.
  • Do not worry about their actual job. Your grandmother does not need to own a shop. You are imagining her as your future boss. What matters is that you know how she thinks about money, risk, and people.

Write a memory, not a description

A description says "he is a careful boss." A memory says where you were, what he did, and what he said. A memory proves you know the person.

Watch it work: Dani, Step 2 Part A
Thin"Ray is the owner. He is careful and cares about safety."
Detailed"Ray Hale has owned the shop for 22 years and I have worked for him for three. Last winter he pulled a customer's truck off the lift halfway through a brake job because he noticed a slow leak nobody else saw. He said, 'We don't send anything out on a maybe.'"
Why the detailed version works: you can picture the moment, and you now know something real about how Ray makes decisions.

Doing this well

  • Write an actual memory: where you were, what they did, and what they said.
  • Include something they really said, in their words or close to it.
  • Say what they would ask first if you pitched them something. That question tells you what they care about most. For Ray, it might be: "What happens to my schedule while this gets installed?"

If you cannot remember a real moment with this person, you have not picked someone you know well enough yet. Pick someone else.

Where this goes: Like Step 1, this does not get its own section. It shapes how the whole paper is written, because you are writing it to convince this person.

Name the real person you will use as your supervisor. Note how you know them, one moment you remember with them including something they said, and anything about them you would need to confirm.

How to write good notes
  • Pick someone you could describe without guessing. If you cannot remember a real moment with them, pick someone else.
  • Write a memory, not a description: where you were, what they did, and what they said. Put in a quote, even a rough one.
  • List one thing you would need to confirm before you rely on it, like how long they have held the role or what they decide on their own.
Aim for at least 50 words.

Section D: Step 2 of 6, Part B

Your Supervisor: how they see work and where they lean

Next, Step 2 asks you to describe how your person thinks about work. You do this three ways, and each one ties back to an earlier module.

Their paradigm (Module 1)

A paradigm is the set of beliefs and assumptions a person uses to decide what is normal, what matters, and what the right way to do work looks like. Think of it as the lens they see everything through. When you read about Challenger and Columbia, you saw what happens when a workplace's lens gets locked in. In the worksheet you pick one from this list:

Culture of Production: getting output out the door comes first. Speed, volume, and deadlines drive decisions. You hear: "We have to get this out today."
Culture of Safety: preventing harm comes first. People are expected to stop work and speak up when something looks risky. You hear: "If it feels wrong, stop."
Normalization of Deviance: a shortcut once questioned slowly becomes "how we do it" because nothing bad has happened yet. You hear: "We have always done it this way and nothing happened."
Structural Secrecy: information gets lost or filtered because of how the organization is set up. You hear: "Nobody told me about that."
Culture of Compliance: rules, policies, and regulations drive decisions. You hear: "What does the policy say?"
Culture of Cost-Containment: money is the main lens. Every request has to prove it saves or earns more than it costs. You hear: "What does it cost, and what does it save?"
Just Culture / Learning Culture: mistakes are a chance to learn and fix the system, not just a reason to punish. You hear: "What can we change so this does not happen again?"
Command Culture: authority and chain of command drive decisions. You hear: "Who signed off on this?"

Their bias (Module 2)

A bias is a lean. Everybody has one. A paradigm is the lens they see work through. A bias is a mental shortcut that tilts their judgment, often without them noticing. In the worksheet you pick one from this list:

Confirmation Bias: favoring information that matches what you already believe.
Status Quo Bias: keeping things as they are because change feels risky or like extra work.
Authority Bias: weighing an idea by who said it instead of the reasoning behind it.
Sunk Cost Bias: sticking with something because of time or money already put in, even when it is not working.
Risk Aversion Bias: choosing the familiar option over a better one because the unknown feels too uncertain.
Halo Effect: assuming something is good overall because one part impressed you, like a brand name.
Anchoring Bias: getting stuck on the first number you hear and judging everything after it.
Availability Bias: judging how common a problem is by how recently or vividly you experienced it.
Optimism Bias: assuming things will turn out fine and underestimating the odds something goes wrong.

When you pick one in the worksheet, a definition appears with an "Ask yourself" question. Read it. Then answer by pointing to a real moment. A label by itself proves nothing. The moment proves it.

Their identity (Module 3)

Last, name their generation or cultural background and connect it to the memory you wrote. Then say what matters most to them. The finished profile should read like one person, not a list of labels.

Watch it work: Dani, Step 2 Part B

Paradigm: Culture of Safety. "Ray pulled the truck off the lift on a maybe. He has said more than once, 'Nobody gets hurt in my shop.' When we got a cheaper scanner last year he made me test it on three cars before he would trust it."

Bias: Risk Aversion Bias. "He kept our old shop computer for six years after it started freezing because he did not want to learn a new system. Ray picks the safe, familiar option, even when a better one is sitting right there."

Identity: "Ray is in his late fifties and started as a mechanic in the nineties, when you learned by watching someone older. He trusts people who have been proven, and he trusts brands he has seen work."

What matters most: "That nobody gets hurt and that the bays stay booked."

What this tells Dani about the pitch: lead with safety, address Ray's fear of the unfamiliar with a warranty, a trial, and local service, and bring names of other shops that already use the machine. Each answer points to a real moment.
Where this goes: This also has no section of its own. It tells you how to shape your pitch for this person.

  • For each label, write the moment or quote that backs it up. A label by itself proves nothing.
  • Add one thing that might make you wrong. It shows you are testing your read, not just defending it.

Section E: Step 3 of 6

The Problem and the Ask

Now you get to the point. Step 3 asks for three things: a specific incident, a specific cost, and one clear sentence that says what you want.

Vague versions are easy to write and easy to dismiss. "Our equipment is old" gets a shrug. A specific incident with a number gets attention.

1. The problem, with the incident that proves it

Describe the last time it actually happened. When it happened, what happened, and who or what it affected. Then put numbers on it: hours waited, jobs turned away, dollars lost, vehicles backed up.

No numbers handy? Estimate honestly and say it is an estimate. "About six hours" is fine. You can count from memory, check a schedule, a log, or a receipt, or ask a coworker. Do not invent a number and present it as fact.

2. The cost of doing nothing

If nothing changes, what gets worse over the next few months, and roughly when? "It keeps being a problem" does not count. Take the incident and ask how often it could happen and what that adds up to.

3. The ask

Say exactly what you want approved. Name the exact item, and say what decision you need. After one read, your supervisor should know what to approve.

Watch it work: Dani, Step 3
Dani's first draftProblem: "The rack is a bottleneck." Cost: "It slows us down." Ask: "I think we need better alignment equipment."

Dani's revision:

Problem: "Last Tuesday our only alignment rack was down for six hours for a hydraulic fix. We turned away four alignment jobs. At about $160 each, that is roughly $640 gone in one day."

Cost of doing nothing: "A rack goes down about once a month. At $640 a time, that is about $7,700 a year in turned-away work, and some of those customers do not come back."

Ask: "I am asking you to approve buying a second alignment machine, around $4,000, so the shop is never down to one rack."

Why it works: one specific incident, numbers that add up, a yearly cost that shows what waiting costs, and an ask that names the item, the price, and the decision.

Doing this well

  • Describe one specific incident: when it happened, what happened, and who or what it affected.
  • If you work in your industry, use a real incident. If you do not have experience yet, imagine a realistic one in your industry and say that it is imagined.
  • Put numbers on it: hours waited, jobs turned away, dollars lost, vehicles backed up.
  • In the cost of doing nothing, say what gets worse and roughly when.
  • Make your ask name the exact item. After one read, your supervisor should know what to approve.
Where this goes: This becomes the start of the Introduction in your final paper: the problem you are solving and why it matters.

    Tip: Look for the one with no date, no number, and no incident.

Jot down a problem and the incident behind it. If you work in your industry, use a real one. If you do not have experience yet, imagine a realistic scenario in your industry and say that it is imagined. Include the date, who or what it affected, and a number (an honest estimate is fine).

How to write good notes
  • Pick one incident. Use a real one if you have industry experience, or imagine one that could really happen in your field. If it is imagined, say so.
  • Give its date, what happened, and who or what it affected.
  • Put at least one number on it. If you are not sure, estimate honestly and say it is an estimate.

Section F: Step 4 of 6

The Situation on the Ground

Step 4 is about the circumstances around your request. A supervisor never says yes in a vacuum. They think about money, timing, and history. It has three quick questions.

  • Budget cycle. Is there a known budget window or funding cycle that affects this request? Some places buy equipment once a year. Some buy whenever there is cash. Knowing which helps you time the ask.
  • Timing pressure. Is there a deadline or scheduling reason this needs to happen soon? A busy season, a contract, a safety inspection, a warranty that is running out.
  • Precedent. Has a similar purchase happened before, at your workplace or a comparable one? If yes, how did it go? If no, why not?

Either a yes or a no can be right. What counts is the explanation. A yes or no by itself is only the start.

Watch it work: Dani, Step 4

Budget cycle: No. "Ray does not run a yearly equipment budget. He buys equipment one piece at a time out of shop profit, usually after a good month. That means my pitch has to show how quickly the machine pays for itself."

Timing pressure: Yes. "Winter tire season starts in October and our alignment work doubles. Last year we were backed up almost every Saturday from October to January."

Precedent: Yes. "Three years ago Ray bought a new tire machine for about $5,000. He told me it paid for itself in about a year. He still brings that up when we talk about equipment."

Why it works: each answer has a fact only this workplace would have. Each one also tells Dani something about how to build the pitch.

Doing this well

  • Back up every answer with facts: dates, dollar amounts, names of past purchases, deadlines.
  • A "no" is a perfectly good answer. Explain it with real facts about your own workplace, school, or team.
  • Test each answer: could it apply to any workplace? If so, rewrite it until it could only be about yours.
Where this goes: This feeds the Introduction and your final recommendation. It explains why your request matters now and whether the timing works.

Check what you read

How to write good notes
  • For each of the three questions, write what you already know. A date, a dollar amount, a past purchase, or a deadline all count.
  • Write what you would need to check and who would know, like a budget owner or the person who bought the last piece of equipment.
  • Pick the one you are most sure about and say why. If you are working from an imagined scenario, go with what you would expect to be true in your industry.
Aim for at least 40 words.

Section G: Step 5 of 6

The Equipment or Tool

Step 5 is where you name what you are proposing. Lay out what you have now next to what you want instead. Then pick features and explain why each one matters to the problem you described in Step 3.

What you have now

Describe it in measurable terms. How many, how often it is booked, how long a job takes, how old it is. "Our current rack is 15 years old, booked about six hours a day, and an alignment takes about 50 minutes" is something a supervisor can picture.

What you are proposing

Name the actual item, with brand and model if you know it. If you do not know yet, say what type of equipment it is. You will research it in Week 2, so it is okay to make your best starting guess now.

Features, tied to your problem

A feature is a real spec or capability. "Better" and "newer" are not features. Every feature you list needs a "why it matters" that ties back to the exact problem from Step 3, using the same details.

Watch it work: Dani, Step 5

Now: "One 15-year-old alignment rack, booked about six hours a day. An alignment takes about 50 minutes."

Proposing: "A second alignment machine in the $4,000 range. The exact model comes from Week 2 research."

Feature 1: "Runs a full alignment in about 20 minutes. Why it matters: the rack is blocked less, so on a day like last Tuesday we are not turning away four jobs."

Feature 2: "Comes with a multi-year warranty and local service. Why it matters: Ray worries about being stuck with a machine nobody will fix. This answers it before he asks."

Why it works: each feature is a real capability, and each "why it matters" points to the Step 3 problem and to what Ray cares about from Step 2. The pitch fits together.

Doing this well

  • Name the actual item, with brand and model if you know it, and describe what you have now in measurable terms.
  • Make each feature a real spec or capability. "Better" and "newer" do not count.
  • Tie each "why it matters" back to the exact problem from Step 3.
  • Pick features you can explain. Every feature you list, expect a question about it later.
Where this goes: This is the product you will research in Week 2. What you learn about it ends up in the Review of Literature and the Methodology.

Section H: Step 6 of 6

The Rules of the Proposal (Genre and Constraints)

Every workplace has rules for how a proposal is written and how long it can be. Step 6 is where you learn yours. This step is already filled in for you in the worksheet, so your job is to understand it.

The fancy words are genre and constraints. Genre means the kind of document it is, a workplace proposal. Constraints means the limits, like length and due date.

Tone

Plain, direct, and professional. Write the way you would explain this to your supervisor in person: clear, specific, and no fancy words.

Too fancy"It is imperative that the establishment procure additional alignment instrumentation."
Plain and direct"We need a second alignment machine so we stop turning away jobs."

Structure

The proposal has five sections, in this order: Introduction, Review of Literature, Methodology, Conclusion, References.

Length and due date

Around 3 to 4 pages. Due before your final defense meeting.

Where each piece ends up

Over three weeks you build your proposal one piece at a time. At the end, the pieces get put together in the order above. You do not need to memorize it. Just know where what you are doing now ends up.

Introduction (Weeks 1 and 2): the problem you are solving, why it matters, what you expect to find, and the kind of research you will do. It starts with your problem and ask from this week.
Review of Literature (Week 2): the research you do about who is affected, the workplace principle behind it, and where it happens.
Methodology (Week 3): how you gathered your evidence, what could have skewed it, and whether it applies beyond your own workplace.
Conclusion (Week 3): did the evidence back up what you expected? Said in your own words, based on what you found.
